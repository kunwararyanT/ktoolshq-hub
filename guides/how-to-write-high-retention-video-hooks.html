How to Write and Test a Strong Opening Hook for Short Videos

An opening hook gives viewers a reason to understand what a short video will show. It does not guarantee that someone will keep watching: topic, delivery, pacing, audience fit and the video itself all matter. The useful goal is to make the subject clear quickly and then deliver what the opening promises.

Build a hook from the actual video

  1. Name the subject. Let the viewer understand what they are looking at or what question the clip answers.
  2. Choose one reason to continue. This might be a surprising result, a comparison, a practical problem, a reveal or a question answered by the clip.
  3. Make the promise specific. Avoid claims that the footage does not support.
  4. Start close to the event. Remove setup that the viewer does not need to understand the clip.
  5. Deliver the payoff. If the opening suggests a result, comparison or reveal, make sure the video actually resolves it.

Examples you can adapt

  • Demonstration: “I tried this setting on the same clip—here is what changed.”
  • Before and after: “Watch the background disappear from this photo.”
  • Comparison: “These two exports look similar, but compare the file sizes.”
  • Problem and fix: “If the caption gets covered by the buttons, move it before you export.”
  • Experiment: “I tested the two options on the same device; here is the result.”

These are starting structures, not proven universal templates. Use only an example that matches the footage and does not mislead viewers.

Use the Hook Scorer appropriately

HookCraft applies a set of JavaScript rules and word/phrase checks to the text you enter. It is a drafting aid, not an AI audience simulator. Its score cannot measure delivery, visuals, pacing, credibility, viewer satisfaction or future distribution. A lower score can still accompany a good hook, and a high score does not prove that a video will perform well.

Test with your own channel data

When comparing openings, hold the topic and editing style as consistent as practical, then review the analytics available for each platform. Look at retention or viewed-versus-swiped metrics where available, but account for sample size, audience differences and posting context. Do not attribute a result to the first sentence alone when the video, timing and audience also differ.

Common mistakes

  • Opening with an exaggerated claim that the clip does not prove.
  • Using several questions before giving the viewer any context.
  • Keeping a long intro, logo animation or greeting before the central event.
  • Writing a hook that promises one topic, then switching to another.
  • Chasing a score or copying a trend instead of matching the hook to the actual content.

Review your opening with the audio muted and with sound on, check that captions are readable, and make sure the first few seconds accurately represent what follows.
